## Privacy

The basics I follow online: start the browser in private mode, use a search engine that does not profile you such as [DuckDuckGo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DuckDuckGo) or Startpage, run a tracker blocker such as [Privacy Badger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Privacy_Badger) and an ad blocker such as uBlock Origin, clear cache and history on exit, insist on HTTPS, encrypt DNS with a service such as [NextDNS](https://nextdns.io/) or [DNSCrypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DNSCrypt), route traffic through a VPN, and keep anti-malware current.

### Why we watch

We study, monitor, and surveil for a short list of reasons: curiosity, to understand a thing; maintenance, to improve it; fear, to catch changes or threats early; discovery, because close study turns up the unexpected; decisions, since policy should rest on evidence; money, because knowing a market or a competitor pays; national security and public safety; and passion, since infatuation and love are their own kind of watching. Most surveillance debates are really arguments about which of these reasons is in play and whether it justifies the intrusion.

### Overreach

When a thorough evaluation finds no threat and an agency keeps monitoring a person anyway, that is overreach, and in the United States it runs into the [Fourth Amendment](https://constitution.congress.gov/constitution/amendment-4/) on unreasonable searches and the [First Amendment](https://constitution.congress.gov/constitution/amendment-1/) on speech and association. Surveillance without evidence or reasonable suspicion is an infringement of those rights, whatever legal label it ends up with. Suing over it is possible but hard: you have to show that the monitoring exceeded legal bounds, that it violated a constitutional protection, and that no reasonable basis for continuing it existed.
