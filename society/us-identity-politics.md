## U.S. Identity Politics

[Identity politics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Identity_politics) is not a recent infection. Identity has always organized the U.S. party system; what changed is visibility and [polarization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_polarization_in_the_United_States). The Democratic coalition gathers minority status, urban life, higher education, and cosmopolitan values, bound by a shared moral self-image of inclusion. The Republican coalition gathers cultural continuity, national belonging, religion, and perceived loss of status, with appeals to "real Americans" and "traditional values" as its markers. Partisan sorting has compressed many identities into one label, so disagreement lands as personal offense. Social media pours gasoline on this by rewarding outrage and certainty and punishing nuance; the mechanism is in the [monetization model](monetization-model.md).

### My take

U.S. politics is identity-first with policy rationalized afterward. Parties sell moral stories to different kinds of people, and voters buy the one that flatters their self-image and fears; once identity is engaged, reason becomes a lawyer, not a judge. My impression of [Donald Trump](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_Trump)'s supporters, not survey data, is that they want rid of what they see afflicting the country, fear immigration and the loss of their rights, and see the opposition as entirely corrupt, and that caricature has consequences. The exit is a centrist coalition that rejects tribal loyalty as a virtue, borrows working ideas from both sides, and treats disagreement as information rather than heresy. Individually, opt out: refuse to signal, caricature, or dehumanize, since every act of performative outrage feeds a machine that is paid to keep the temperature up.
